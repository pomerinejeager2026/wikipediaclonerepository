The monarch butterfly or simply monarch (Danaus plexippus) is a milkweed butterfly in the family Nymphalidae. It is among the most recognizable butterflies in North America, known for its bright orange wings patterned with black veins and white-spotted borders.[1]

The monarch is best known for its long-distance seasonal migration, in which eastern North American populations travel thousands of kilometres to overwinter in the mountain forests of central Mexico. Its caterpillars feed only on milkweeds, which makes both larvae and adults distasteful to many predators.[2][3]

Description[edit]

Adults have a wingspan of roughly 9–10 cm. The upper side of the wings is tawny orange, crossed by black veins, with a black margin carrying two rows of small white spots. Males can be distinguished from females by a small black scent patch on a vein of each hindwing and by their slightly thinner wing veins.[1]

Like other members of the subfamily Danainae, the monarch appears to stand on four legs; the front pair is reduced and held tucked against the body.[citation needed]

Life cycle[edit]

A fifth-instar larva feeding on a milkweed leaf

The monarch undergoes complete metamorphosis, passing through four stages: egg, larva (caterpillar), pupa (chrysalis), and adult. Females lay single eggs on the underside of milkweed leaves. The larva grows through five instars, shedding its skin between each, before forming a pale green chrysalis flecked with gold.[2]

Adults of summer generations typically live only a few weeks. The final generation of the year, which makes the autumn migration, enters a state of reproductive diapause and can live for several months.[4]

Host plants[edit]

Larvae feed almost exclusively on plants in the genus Asclepias (milkweeds). Adults are less selective and take nectar from a wide range of flowering plants, including goldenrods, asters, and blazing stars.[3]

Migration[edit]

Each autumn, monarchs east of the Rocky Mountains fly south toward a small number of high-altitude oyamel fir forests in central Mexico, where millions cluster on tree trunks and branches through the winter. Most western monarchs instead overwinter in groves along the coast of California.[4]

No single butterfly completes the full round trip. The northward return in spring is carried out over several successive generations, so the individuals that reach the northern breeding range are the great-grandchildren of those that left it.[4] Researchers have shown that migrating monarchs navigate using a time-compensated sun compass.[5]

Defense against predators[edit]

Milkweeds contain toxic cardiac glycosides (cardenolides), which larvae store in their bodies. These compounds persist into adulthood and make monarchs unpalatable or emetic to many birds. The butterfly's vivid coloration serves as a warning signal, an example of aposematism.[2]

The similar-looking viceroy (Limenitis archippus) was long cited as a Batesian mimic of the monarch, but later studies found the viceroy is itself distasteful, making the relationship closer to Müllerian mimicry.[6]

Conservation[edit]

Overwintering counts of both eastern and western populations have declined substantially since the 1990s. Contributing factors include loss of milkweed habitat in breeding areas, degradation of overwintering forests, pesticide use, and extreme weather events.[3]

  • Planting native milkweed and nectar plants in gardens and roadsides
  • Protection of overwintering sites in Mexico and California
  • Citizen-science tagging and population monitoring programs
